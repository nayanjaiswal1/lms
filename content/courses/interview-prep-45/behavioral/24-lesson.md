## Why interviewers ask this

This is different from the Day 4 conflict story, which was about disagreeing on a technical decision. This one is about personality and working-style friction: someone who was dismissive, took credit, or missed commitments repeatedly. Interviewers want to know you can stay professional and keep shipping when you can't just out-argue the problem with data.

## The framework: STAR, weighted on professionalism under friction

- **Situation**: the specific behavior pattern, described factually, not as a character assassination.
- **Task**: how their behavior was actually affecting your work or the team's.
- **Action**: what you did: usually a direct, private conversation first, escalation only if that didn't work, and continuing to collaborate professionally regardless of your personal feelings.
- **Result**: the outcome, even if only partial. Not every difficult-coworker story ends in a full turnaround, and pretending it does can read as dishonest.

The trap here is nearly identical to Day 4: don't make the other person a villain, and don't spend the story venting. Interviewers are evaluating your behavior, not judging theirs.

## Worked example

> **Situation**: "I worked with an engineer on another team who'd regularly commit to API contract changes in meetings, then not follow through, which broke my team's integration work without warning at least three times over two months. **Task**: I needed the integration to actually stay stable, and escalating immediately felt like it would burn a relationship I'd need long-term. **Action**: I asked him directly, one-on-one, whether the commitments in meetings were realistic or if something upstream was blocking him. It turned out his team was chronically understaffed and he was over-committing under pressure from his own manager. I suggested we move from verbal commitments to a shared doc with actual dates, so at minimum I'd get advance warning instead of a broken integration. **Result**: The surprise breakages stopped. He still occasionally missed dates, but now I had lead time to adjust instead of finding out from a failing test. I didn't fix his team's staffing problem, but I fixed the part of it that was actually my problem to fix."

The honest "I didn't fix everything, but I fixed my piece of it" ending is more credible than a fake full resolution.

## Your template

```
Situation: [the specific friction, described factually]
Task: [how it was actually affecting your work]
Action: [private conversation first] → [what you learned/proposed] → [escalation only if needed]
Result: [the outcome, even if partial], no invented full resolution
```

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Describe behavior factually, not as a personality attack | Turn the story into venting about the person |
| Show you tried a direct conversation before escalating | Jump straight to going over their head |
| Admit if the resolution was only partial | Fabricate a tidy happy ending |
