## Why interviewers ask this

These three questions aren't small talk. Each is a probe for a specific risk. "Why are you looking to change?" checks whether you're running toward something or fleeing a mess (or worse, fleeing yourself). "What are you looking for in a new role?" checks whether your criteria are self-aware and actually match what the role offers. "Greatest strength/weakness" checks self-awareness and whether you can name a real limitation without false modesty or a thinly disguised brag.

## The framework: one honest reason, one proof point, no red flags

Each answer should run 30-45 seconds: state the answer plainly, back it with one concrete detail, then stop.

**Why are you looking to change?** Pick a reason about growth, not escape: scope, scale, technology, or ownership you don't have today. Never bad-mouth a current employer or manager, even if it's true. It reads as a risk you'll say the same about this company in two years.

**What are you looking for in a new role?** Name 2-3 concrete criteria (team size, stack, ownership level, mentorship) and tie at least one directly to something this specific role offers. Vague answers like "good culture" or "growth opportunities" sound like you haven't thought about it.

**Greatest strength/weakness?** Strength: pick one relevant to the role and back it with a specific example, more than just an adjective. Weakness: pick something real and specific, then describe the concrete system you use to manage it. "I'm working on it" alone isn't a system.

## Worked example

> **Why are you looking to change?** "I've grown a lot at my current company, but it's a 12-person engineering team and I've hit the point where the interesting technical problems, the ones at real scale, don't exist here yet. I want to work somewhere with more traffic and more engineers to learn from."
>
> **What are you looking for?** "Three things: a codebase with enough scale that performance actually matters, a team where I can learn from senior engineers instead of being the most senior person in the room, and ownership of a problem end-to-end rather than a slice of a ticket queue. This role's backend rewrite project looks like exactly that."
>
> **Greatest weakness?** "I default to solving problems myself before asking for input, which works fine solo but slows a team down. I've missed chances to get feedback earlier. I've started forcing myself to post a design doc before writing code, even a rough one, specifically so someone can catch a bad assumption before I've sunk two days into it."

## Your template

```
Why changing: I've [grown/learned X] at my current role, but [specific thing missing: scale, scope, stack, ownership]. I want [specific thing], which is why I'm looking at [this kind of role/company].

Looking for: [criterion 1], [criterion 2], and [criterion 3], plus [specific reason this role fits one of them].

Strength: [trait], shown when [specific example].
Weakness: [real, specific limitation]. I manage it by [concrete system/habit].
```

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Frame "why changing" around growth, not escape | Criticize your current employer or manager |
| Name concrete, role-relevant criteria | Say "good culture" or "growth opportunities" with no detail |
| Pick a weakness that's real and specific | Disguise a brag as a weakness |
| Show a concrete system for managing the weakness | Just say "I'm working on it" with no mechanism |
