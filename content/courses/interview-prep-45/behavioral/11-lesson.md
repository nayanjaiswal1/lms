## Why interviewers ask this

"Tell me about a time you had too much work" is checking whether you have a real prioritization method or just work longer hours when the pile grows. Every engineer above junior level juggles competing demands from multiple stakeholders. Interviewers want to see a repeatable decision process, not a one-off story about a heroic all-nighter.

## The framework: STAR, plus naming a real prioritization method

Same STAR shape as prior days. What separates a strong answer here is naming the actual mechanism you used to decide what came first. Vague "I just prioritized" answers are the weakest version of this story.

Two frameworks worth knowing by name, so you can reference them credibly:

- **Impact vs. effort**: plot each task on impact delivered against effort required. Do high-impact/low-effort first, defer low-impact/high-effort.
- **Eisenhower (urgent vs. important)**: separate what's urgent from what's actually important. Too much reactive work is a sign you're only doing urgent, not important, work.

Pick whichever one actually matches how you reasoned through the real situation. Don't retrofit a framework name onto a decision you made by gut feel: interviewers will ask how you weighed the trade-off, and a fabricated framework falls apart under a follow-up question.

## Worked example

> **Situation**: "In the same week, I had a critical bug fix for a paying customer, a code review backlog blocking two teammates, and a feature deadline for a demo to leadership. **Task**: I had maybe 20 hours of real work condensed into a 3-day window and needed to decide what actually got done. **Action**: I used a rough impact-vs-effort read. The bug fix was high impact (revenue-affecting customer) and moderate effort, so it went first. The code reviews were high impact too, since they were blocking two other people's work rather than only mine, and they were low effort besides, maybe 40 minutes total, so I did those same-day rather than letting them compound into a bigger blocker. The demo feature was high visibility, but when I actually talked to my manager, the deadline had slack I didn't know about: leadership's demo wasn't until the following week, not that Friday. I flagged that ambiguity instead of assuming, which freed up a full day. **Result**: The customer bug was fixed within 4 hours, my teammates were unblocked the same day, and the demo feature shipped a day ahead of its real deadline with time to polish it. The main lesson was that the deadline I was most stressed about turned out to be the one I hadn't actually confirmed."

The confirming-the-deadline move is the real skill: don't just prioritize against assumptions, check them.

## Your template

```
Situation: [the competing demands, specific enough to be real]
Task: [what you had to decide]
Action: [the framework or reasoning you used to sequence the work]
Result: [what got done, in what order] + [what you learned about your own assumptions]
```

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Name the actual reasoning you used to sequence work | Say "I just prioritized" with no method |
| Show you communicated trade-offs to stakeholders | Silently drop a commitment without telling anyone |
| Mention confirming an assumption that turned out wrong | Only tell the story where your first instinct was right |
