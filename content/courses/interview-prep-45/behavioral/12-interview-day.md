## Remote interview checklist

Lock this down before the interview, not during it:

| Item | Check |
|---|---|
| Laptop charger, or a backup power source, battery not below 80% | Plugged in during the interview regardless |
| Internet | Wired if possible; if wifi-only, know your backup, like a phone hotspot tested in advance |
| Camera framing | Face centered, decent lighting, facing a window or lamp, not backlit |
| Microphone | Tested in an actual call, background noise like fans or notifications checked |
| Screen share or IDE setup | Whatever tool they specified, opened and tested once beforehand |
| Second monitor or notes | Confirm it's allowed; some platforms flag a second screen as a red flag |
| Notifications | Slack, email, and phone notifications off for the interview window |
| Water | Within reach, not something you have to get up for |

> **Remember:** a dead microphone or a dying laptop costs composure in the exact minutes that matter most.

## In-person interview checklist

Pick your outfit and lay it out the night before; one notch more formal than the stated dress code is the safe default. Check your route or commute with real-time traffic and a buffer, aiming to arrive 10-15 minutes early, not more. Have your photo ID and any requested documents ready, and silence your phone before entering the building.

## Universal prep, whatever the format

Regardless of remote or in person: have your resume and the job description printed or saved offline, keep your prepared questions to ask accessible without fumbling for them, and know the interview panel structure if it's been shared, since knowing what's coming reduces uncertainty-driven anxiety. If remote and the company is in a different time zone, confirm the interview time explicitly rather than assuming.

**In the last 24 hours before the first interview**, make sure your environment is tested: mic, camera, IDE or whiteboard tool, a stable connection, or route and parking details if you're going onsite. Save the login link or address somewhere visible 15 minutes before. Keep water, a notepad, and a pen within reach, and open your resume and any take-home materials in a tab. Set an alarm with a real buffer, aiming to be ready 15 minutes early.

> **Remember:** logging in or arriving late is the one interview-day mistake you generally can't recover from. Everything else on this checklist exists to prevent that one thing.

## The mindset that actually helps once you're in the room

Reframe the interview as a working session, not an exam: the interviewer is trying to find a reason to hire you. Think out loud from the first minute. Silence is the one thing interviewers consistently rate as a bad signal, even when the final answer turns out correct, because they're grading your process as much as your output.

Ask clarifying questions before you code or design: input size, duplicates, and edge cases for a coding problem; scale numbers, the read-to-write ratio, and consistency requirements for a system design. This is expected, not a weakness. When you're stuck, say so out loud ("I think there's an edge case I'm missing around X, let me think through it") instead of typing silently and guessing, and ask for a nudge directly if you need one. Don't apologize for thinking; a short "let me think for a second" is enough.

Before the first interview, do a 5-minute warm-up: one easy problem, or explaining one system out loud, not new material. Sit in a quiet room with your phone on silent and out of reach.

> **Remember:** silence reads worse than a wrong guess said out loud. Narrate your thinking even when you're stuck.

## The between-rounds ritual

Do this immediately after each interview, before anything else, since memory of what was actually asked decays fast:

```
Company / Round:
Interviewer role:
Questions asked:
My approach:
What went well:
What I'd adjust for next time:
Confidence (1-5):
```

Then physically reset: a short walk, food, water. Avoid spiraling on a rough round (one written adjustment is enough, don't replay it in your head for the next hour) and avoid over-preparing in the gap (cramming a new topic in the 30 minutes before the next interview adds noise, not signal).

**Energy dips in the afternoon are real.** Eat protein rather than something that spikes and crashes 45 minutes later, and hydrate: mild dehydration measurably affects concentration and working memory. If your rounds cover different domains, give yourself two minutes to consciously switch context before the next one starts.

**In the evening**, do a brief review and rest, 15-20 minutes at most. Skim your notes for any question type that came up more than once, and note anything to say differently tomorrow, one line per item. Don't start re-studying a topic tonight because one round exposed a gap; if it comes up again tomorrow, handle it live, the way real interviews actually work. Then protect your rest: screens off, caffeine cutoff respected, sleep protected.

## Common day-of mistakes

- Over-explaining: once you've stated your approach and the interviewer nods, start executing instead of continuing to narrate.
- Freezing on "tell me about yourself" because you never rehearsed a tight 60-90 second version.
- Badmouthing a previous employer or manager, even when asked directly; reframe around what you learned instead.
- Going silent when stuck instead of narrating your thinking, the single most common self-inflicted wound of the day.

> **Remember:** one written adjustment per rough round is enough. Replaying it in your head for the next hour only costs you the next round too.
